## Intuition

When a power changes a little, each repeated factor contributes one leading-order change. The number of factors becomes the coefficient of the derivative.

## Formalism

$$
\frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}
$$

Expand (x+h) to the nth power. After subtracting x to the nth power and dividing by h, only the term with one h survives the limit.

[Explore derivative](concept:derivative). [Explore exponents](concept:exponents).

## Worked example

For f(x)=3x^3, find f prime at x=2.

Start with the defining relationship. Expand (x+h) to the nth power. After subtracting x to the nth power and dividing by h, only the term with one h survives the limit. Substitute the given values: **`12*3` = 36 **. Keep the unit through the calculation and check whether the sign and scale fit the situation.

## Where it breaks

Negative and fractional powers require the original domain. At zero, a fractional power may fail to have a finite derivative.

A common trap is: **reducing the power but forgetting its coefficient**. Count which of the n factors supplied the one small change.
