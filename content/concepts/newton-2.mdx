## Intuition

A force changes motion; it does not maintain motion by default. A cart can keep moving at constant velocity when its forces balance. What an unbalanced force controls is the **rate at which velocity changes**.

Mass measures resistance to that change. Push two carts with the same net force: the cart with twice the mass accelerates half as much. Push the same cart twice as hard, and its acceleration doubles, within this model.

## Formalism

In an inertial frame, the total external force equals the rate of change of momentum. For a constant-mass object this becomes

$$\sum\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}=\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt}=m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}=m\mathbf{a}.$$

The sum matters. Draw a [free-body diagram](concept:free-body), choose axes, and add signed components before dividing by mass. One newton is $1\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}$, so force divided by kilograms has acceleration units.

The mathematical work is [rearranging an equation](concept:equations); the physical work is deciding which forces belong in the sum. Start with [Newton’s first law](concept:newton-1) and the meaning of [acceleration](concept:acceleration).

## Worked example

A $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ cart experiences an $8\,\mathrm{N}$ horizontal push to the right and $2\,\mathrm{N}$ of friction to the left. Take right as positive.

$$F_{\mathrm{net},x}=8\,\mathrm{N}-2\,\mathrm{N}=6\,\mathrm{N},\qquad a_x=\frac{6\,\mathrm{N}}{2\,\mathrm{kg}}=3\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

If the cart initially moves left at $-4\,\mathrm{m/s}$, it still accelerates right. After one second with the same forces, its velocity is $-1\,\mathrm{m/s}$. It is moving left but slowing down. Acceleration and velocity need not point the same way.

**Check the model:** we assumed the forces stay constant over that second. Friction can change when the cart stops or reverses, so extrapolating this calculation indefinitely would need a new force analysis.

## Where it breaks

Using the largest individual force instead of the net force gives the wrong acceleration. A balanced-force object can have nonzero velocity; zero acceleration does not imply rest.

The simple $\mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}$ form here assumes constant mass and an inertial frame. Rockets require careful momentum-flow accounting, and rotating frames need additional apparent-force terms. At relativistic speeds, momentum is not $m\mathbf{v}$.
