## Intuition

Mass farther from an axis is harder to angularly accelerate because the same angular change demands more tangential speed. Rotational inertia weights mass by distance squared.

## Formalism

$$
I=\sum m_ir_i^2,\quad\tau=I\alpha,\quad K_{rot}=\tfrac12I\omega^2
$$

Each mass needs tangential acceleration r alpha and hence force m r alpha. Its torque is m r squared alpha; summing gives I alpha.

[Explore torque](concept:torque). [Explore circular motion](concept:circular-motion). [Explore integral](concept:integral).

## Worked example

A point mass 3 kg is at radius 4 m. Find its rotational inertia.

Start with the defining relationship. Each mass needs tangential acceleration r alpha and hence force m r alpha. Its torque is m r squared alpha; summing gives I alpha. Substitute the given values: **`3*4^2` = 48 kg*m^2**. Keep the unit through the calculation and check whether the sign and scale fit the situation.

## Where it breaks

These scalar formulas assume rotation about a fixed principal axis. General three-dimensional rotation needs an inertia tensor.

A common trap is: **adding masses without radius-squared weighting**. Compare identical masses at radius r and 2r; the latter contributes four times the inertia.
