## Intuition

A rising ball slows down, but its energy has not vanished. In an ideal model, kinetic energy is exchanged for gravitational potential energy. On the way down, the exchange reverses.

This perspective changes the problem. Instead of calculating every instant of the motion, compare the energy accounts at two states. You still need a clearly defined system and a record of any energy crossing its boundary.

## Formalism

When only conservative forces do work, mechanical energy is constant:

$$K_i+U_i=K_f+U_f.$$

Near Earth’s surface, $K=\tfrac12mv^2$ and $U=mgh$ with approximately constant $g$. If nonconservative forces do work on this mechanical account,

$$K_i+U_i+W_{\mathrm{nc}}=K_f+U_f.$$

Friction can reduce mechanical energy while increasing internal energy. Total energy is still conserved when the account includes the relevant surroundings. [Kinetic energy](concept:kinetic-energy), [potential energy](concept:potential-energy) and [work](concept:work) tell you which terms to include.

## Worked example

A $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ object is released from rest $5\,\mathrm{m}$ above the ground. Ignore air resistance and take $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Choose zero potential energy at the ground.

Initially $K_i=0$ and $U_i=2\times9.81\times5=98.1\,\mathrm{J}$. Just before impact $U_f=0$, so

$$\frac12mv^2=mgh\quad\Rightarrow\quad v=\sqrt{2gh}=\sqrt{98.1}\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx9.90\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

The mass cancels because both energy expressions contain the same factor. In this ideal model, doubling mass doubles both available potential energy and the kinetic energy required for a given speed.

**Check the scale:** the square root contains $\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$, so the result has speed units. The calculation gives speed, not the direction of velocity or the time of impact.

## Where it breaks

Mechanical energy is not conserved when drag or friction transfers energy into thermal motion. Add those transfers rather than declaring that energy “disappears.” The approximation $mgh$ also needs nearly constant gravity; it is unsuitable for large orbital height changes.

A zero reference for potential energy is a choice. Only potential differences affect the speed prediction. Energy alone generally cannot supply the direction or timing of motion; use forces and kinematics for those questions.
